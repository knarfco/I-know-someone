Ship cargo and fuel tank cleaning and gas-freeing

Cleaning and ventilating vessel cargo, fuel and slop tanks so a Marine Chemist can certify them safe for entry or hot work.

Also called: tank gas-freeing, cargo tank cleaning, fuel tank cleaning on vessels, tank cleaning for hot work

Licensed, regulated work. Only properly licensed and certified specialists may perform it. Requirements vary by state and agency, so confirm credentials before hiring.

Get quotes See specialists

What it is

Tank cleaning removes sludge, scale and product residue from cargo, bunker, slop and lube oil tanks on ships. Crews first strip remaining liquid, then wash with fixed or portable machines using water, hot water or approved chemicals. Ventilation drives off vapors before anyone enters. Workers then muck out sludge by hand or vacuum truck. A Marine Chemist tests the atmosphere and issues a certificate under NFPA 306 that states whether the space is safe for workers, safe for hot work, or both. Repairs and inspections start only after that certificate is posted.

When you need it

  • Hot work, such as welding or cutting, is planned in or near a tank that held fuel or flammable cargo
  • A vessel is entering dry dock or a repair yard
  • Cargo grade is changing and the next cargo cannot tolerate residue
  • Class or flag-state surveys require internal tank inspection
  • A vessel is going to lay-up or to recycling

Credentials to ask for

  • A Marine Chemist certificate under NFPA 306 before hot work where 29 CFR 1915.14(a) requires one
  • A designated Shipyard Competent Person for ongoing atmospheric testing under 29 CFR 1915.12 and 1915.7
  • Confined and enclosed space training under 29 CFR Part 1915 Subpart B, plus respiratory protection
  • Permits and documentation for transporting and disposing of oily waste; requirements vary by state and port

What the scope includes

  • Review the last three cargoes and the tank plan, then isolate pipelines, heating coils and pumps
  • Strip remaining liquid and wash tanks with fixed or portable machines
  • Ventilate and test for oxygen, flammability and toxic gases
  • Enter, remove sludge and scale, and clean structure to the required standard
  • Arrange Marine Chemist testing and post the certificate at the work site
  • Transfer slops and sludge to a licensed receiver and hand over disposal paperwork

How it is priced

Usually priced per tank or as a lump sum per vessel, with day rates for standby. Cost depends on tank volume, residue type and depth, the number of Marine Chemist visits, and waste disposal volume.

Ask before you hire

  • Which Marine Chemist do you use, and who pays for and schedules the certificate visits?
  • Who is your Shipyard Competent Person, and how often will they retest spaces?
  • Where will slops and sludge go, and will we get receipts or manifests?
  • What rescue arrangements do you have for each entered space?

Questions about ship cargo and fuel tank cleaning and gas-freeing

Is a gas-free certificate the same as a Marine Chemist certificate?

People often use the terms loosely. In U.S. shipyard work, the certificate that matters for hot work is issued by a certified Marine Chemist under NFPA 306 and states specific conditions such as Safe for Workers or Safe for Hot Work. Read the actual designations on the certificate before work starts.

How long does a certificate stay valid?

A certificate reflects conditions at the time of testing and lists its own limits. Conditions can change when work, temperature or ballast changes, so a competent person must keep testing and the Marine Chemist may need to recertify. The certificate must be posted near the work and kept on file after the job.

Can tank washing be done at sea?

Some tank washing is done underway under the vessel's own procedures and international pollution rules. Work that involves entry by outside crews, hot work or shore disposal is normally done alongside a pier or in a yard, where shipyard OSHA rules apply.
