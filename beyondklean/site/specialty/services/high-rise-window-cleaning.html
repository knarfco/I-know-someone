High-rise window cleaning

Exterior window cleaning on tall buildings using rope descent systems, building maintenance units or swing stages, under OSHA and IWCA rules.

Also called: rope descent window cleaning, BMU window cleaning, skyscraper window washing, commercial window cleaning at height

Licensed, regulated work. Only properly licensed and certified specialists may perform it. Requirements vary by state and agency, so confirm credentials before hiring.

Get quotes See specialists

What it is

High-rise window cleaning is done from rope descent systems (bosun's chairs), suspended swing stages or a building maintenance unit (BMU) that travels on the roof. Crews rig from certified roof anchors or davits, lower in vertical drops and clean glass, frames and mullions with water-fed tools or squeegees. Each drop needs a separate fall-arrest lifeline, a ground-level controlled zone and attention to wind. Work follows OSHA 29 CFR 1910.27 for rope descent systems, 1910.66 for powered platforms and the ANSI/IWCA I-14.1 Window Cleaning Safety Standard.

When you need it

  • Routine exterior cleaning on a two- to four-times-per-year schedule
  • Before a property sale, tenant move-in or appraisal
  • After a construction project on or near the building leaves dust on the glass
  • When interior staff can no longer reach exterior glass from inside

Credentials to ask for

  • Written anchorage certification from the building owner as required by OSHA 29 CFR 1910.27(b)
  • Training to ANSI/IWCA I-14.1 and, for rope access methods, SPRAT or IRATA technician certification
  • Powered platform training for BMU or swing-stage work under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.66
  • General liability and workers' compensation coverage that explicitly includes work at height

What the scope includes

  • Review of roof anchors, davits, BMU inspection records and a written rigging plan
  • Setup of ground-level barricades and signage below each drop
  • Rigging of a primary line and an independent fall-arrest line for each worker
  • Cleaning of glass, frames and sills drop by drop, with a rescue plan in place
  • Inspection notes on failed sealant, damaged glass or anchor concerns found during the work

How it is priced

Usually priced per cleaning cycle or per drop, sometimes per pane on smaller buildings. Cost depends on building height, number of drops, access method, glass type and setback or overhang complexity.

Ask before you hire

  • Have you reviewed our current anchor certification, and is any anchor out of date?
  • What is your rescue plan if a worker is suspended and cannot move?
  • What wind speed causes you to stop work?
  • Who supervises on site, and what certifications does that person hold?

Questions about high-rise window cleaning

How often should a high-rise have its windows cleaned?

Many office towers clean exterior glass two to four times a year. Buildings near highways, airports, the coast or active construction may need more frequent cycles. The right schedule depends on how quickly dirt and mineral deposits build up.

What happens if our roof anchors are not certified?

The contractor should not use them. OSHA 1910.27 requires the building owner to provide written assurance that each anchorage was tested and certified before use. You will need a qualified person or engineer to inspect and certify the anchors first.

Is a BMU always safer than rope descent?

Not always. A well-maintained BMU reduces the number of rigging setups, but it has its own inspection and operator requirements. The safer method depends on the building design and the condition of the equipment.
