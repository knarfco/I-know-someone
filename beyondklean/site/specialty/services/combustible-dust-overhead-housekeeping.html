Combustible dust overhead housekeeping

Removal of combustible dust from beams, ledges and structure using approved methods to reduce flash fire and explosion risk.

Also called: combustible dust cleaning, dust explosion hazard cleaning, NFPA 660 housekeeping, fugitive dust cleanup

Licensed, regulated work. Only properly licensed and certified specialists may perform it. Requirements vary by state and agency, so confirm credentials before hiring.

Get quotes See specialists

What it is

Many dusts from wood, grain, sugar, flour, plastics, metals and chemicals can burn or explode when suspended in air. Dust on overhead surfaces is dangerous because a small primary event can shake it loose and cause a much larger secondary explosion. NFPA 660, published in 2025, consolidates earlier standards including NFPA 652, 654, 61, 484 and 664 and sets housekeeping expectations. OSHA enforces through its Combustible Dust National Emphasis Program and general duty clause. Cleaning uses vacuums suitable for the hazard, such as those listed for Class II locations, and limits compressed air blowdown. Ignition sources are controlled during work.

When you need it

  • Dust layers building up on beams, ledges, ducts or equipment
  • A dust hazard analysis (DHA) calls for housekeeping
  • Insurance or OSHA inspection findings
  • After a process upset or dust collector failure

Credentials to ask for

  • Familiarity with NFPA 660 and the facility's dust hazard analysis
  • Vacuum equipment rated for the hazard, such as units listed for Class II hazardous locations where required
  • Hot work and ignition source control procedures
  • MEWP training and fall protection for overhead work

What the scope includes

  • Review of the facility's dust hazard analysis and dust properties
  • Ignition source control and coordination with operations
  • Vacuuming from top down with approved equipment
  • Limited, controlled use of blowdown only where allowed
  • Documentation of areas cleaned and remaining dust depth

How it is priced

Usually priced per shift or per zone, often on a recurring schedule. Dust type, accumulation, height, equipment ratings and shutdown needs drive cost.

Ask before you hire

  • Are your vacuums rated for our dust and our area classification?
  • Have you read our dust hazard analysis?
  • How do you control ignition sources during cleaning?
  • How will you document dust levels after cleaning?

Questions about combustible dust overhead housekeeping

How much dust is too much?

NFPA standards use dust layer thickness and area to assess hazard, and the threshold depends on the dust. Your dust hazard analysis should set the limit for your site.

Can we use compressed air?

Blowdown suspends dust in the air and can create the very cloud you want to avoid. NFPA standards limit it to cases where other methods are not practical and set controls.

Is an ordinary shop vacuum safe?

Often not. A vacuum's motor can ignite dust. Use equipment suited to the hazard and the area classification.
