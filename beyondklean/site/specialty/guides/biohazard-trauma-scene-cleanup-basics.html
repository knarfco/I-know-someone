Biohazard and trauma scene cleanup basics

What biohazard cleanup involves, how the OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens standard applies, waste handling rules and what to ask a cleanup company.

What biohazard cleanup covers

Biohazard cleanup removes blood, bodily fluids and other potentially infectious materials after deaths, injuries, crimes, suicides, unattended deaths or sewage backups. It also covers needles, sharps and contaminated items. In commercial settings these events happen in hotels, offices, stores, schools, vehicles and public spaces. The work involves cleaning, disinfection and, often, removing contaminated building materials such as carpet, subfloor or drywall. Trained cleanup companies handle these jobs because the exposure risk and waste rules go beyond normal janitorial work and routine staff training.

The OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens standard

OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030, applies to employers whose workers can reasonably be expected to contact blood or other potentially infectious materials. It requires a written exposure control plan, engineering and work practice controls, personal protective equipment, hepatitis B vaccination offered to exposed workers, post-exposure evaluation and follow-up, labeling, and initial and annual training. A cleanup company should be able to show its exposure control plan and training records. Facility employers whose staff may respond to small incidents also need to meet the standard.

How cleanup is done

A qualified cleanup company first secures the area and assesses how far contamination has spread, since fluids can soak into porous materials and travel under flooring. Workers wear PPE suited to the job. Gross contamination is removed, contaminated porous materials are cut out, and surfaces are cleaned and then disinfected with EPA-registered products used according to label directions and contact times. Some companies use ATP testing to check surface cleanliness. Odor control may follow, and some jobs need a final walkthrough with the owner or insurer. The work area should be left safe for repair crews and occupants.

Waste handling

Biohazard waste must be packaged, labeled and disposed of properly. Under OSHA, contaminated sharps go into closable, puncture-resistant containers, and other regulated waste goes into labeled or color-coded containers. States define and regulate medical waste, including how it is stored, transported and treated, and many require transport by registered haulers. Ask the cleanup company how it handles waste, which licensed transporter and treatment facility it uses, and whether you will receive disposal documentation. Keep those records with the incident file in case regulators, insurers or attorneys ask for them later.

Choosing a cleanup company

Look for a company that does this work regularly and can respond quickly at any hour. Ask for its exposure control plan, training and vaccination records, insurance certificates and any state registration or license that applies. Some states register or license trauma scene cleanup companies. Ask how it works with law enforcement and insurers and whether it bills insurance directly. Pricing typically depends on the size of the affected area, the materials that must be removed, waste volume and labor hours. The company should also treat the site and people involved with discretion.

Checklist

  • Restrict access to the area until cleanup is complete
  • Confirm law enforcement has released the scene when relevant
  • Ask for the company's exposure control plan and training records
  • Confirm state registration or licensing where required
  • Require EPA-registered disinfectants used per label contact times
  • Get documentation of waste transport and treatment
  • Clarify insurance billing and documentation needs
