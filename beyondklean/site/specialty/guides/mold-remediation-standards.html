Mold remediation standards: IICRC S520, EPA guidance, assessment vs remediation

How mold remediation is defined by IICRC S520 and EPA guidance, why assessment and remediation are separate, and what a sound project looks like.

The main references

Most mold remediation in the United States follows two main references. The ANSI/IICRC S520 Standard for Professional Mold Remediation, now in its 2024 fourth edition, sets procedures for inspection, containment, removal, cleaning and post-remediation verification in residential, commercial and institutional buildings. EPA's guide, Mold Remediation in Schools and Commercial Buildings, gives practical guidance on planning and containment based on the size of the affected area. Some local agencies, such as the New York City health department, publish their own guidelines. Specifications often name one or more of these documents.

Moisture is the root cause

Mold grows where there is moisture. Removing mold without fixing the water source leads to regrowth. Every remediation plan should identify the source, such as roof leaks, plumbing failures, condensation or flooding, and schedule repairs. Wet materials should be dried, and porous materials with significant growth, such as drywall, ceiling tiles and insulation, are usually removed rather than cleaned. Non-porous and semi-porous materials can often be cleaned. Biocides are not a substitute for physical removal, and EPA guidance generally does not recommend them as a routine step.

Assessment versus remediation

Assessment and remediation are different jobs. An assessor, often an industrial hygienist or indoor environmental professional, inspects the building, identifies moisture and growth, may take samples and writes a remediation protocol. A remediation contractor follows that protocol to remove the contamination. Using separate firms avoids a conflict of interest, since the company doing the work is not deciding how much work is needed or whether it is finished. Some states, including Texas and Florida, license assessors and remediators separately, and Texas restricts the same company from doing both on one project.

Containment and worker protection

The amount of containment depends on the size and location of the contaminated area and on who occupies the building. Small areas may need only limited protection, while larger areas need full containment with polyethylene barriers, negative air pressure and HEPA air filtration. Workers wear respirators and protective clothing suited to the work. In hospitals and other sensitive buildings, containment is usually stricter. OSHA rules on respiratory protection, 29 CFR 1910.134, apply when respirators are used, including fit testing and medical evaluation.

Clearance and closeout

When removal and cleaning are done, the area should be checked before it is rebuilt or reoccupied. Post-remediation verification may include visual inspection, moisture readings and, if the protocol calls for it, air or surface sampling by an independent assessor. Agree on the clearance criteria before work starts. Closeout documents should include the protocol, photos, moisture readings, clearance results and waste disposal notes. Keep them for building records, insurance claims and future property transactions. If growth returns, these records help show whether the cause is a new moisture problem or an incomplete earlier job.

Checklist

  • Find and fix the moisture source
  • Hire an independent assessor to write a remediation protocol
  • Check your state's mold licensing rules
  • Require containment matched to the size and location of the problem
  • Remove porous materials with significant growth
  • Agree on clearance criteria before work starts
  • Keep the protocol, photos, readings and clearance results on file
