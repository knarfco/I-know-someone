Kitchen exhaust hood cleaning and NFPA 96

What NFPA 96 says about kitchen exhaust inspection and cleaning, how often systems need it, and what a complete cleaning includes.

Why kitchen exhaust systems are cleaned

Cooking releases grease vapor that condenses in hoods, filters, ducts and exhaust fans. Over time this buildup becomes fuel, and a fire that starts on the cooking line can spread into the duct and through the building. NFPA 96, Standard for Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations, sets requirements for the design, inspection and cleaning of these systems. Many local fire codes adopt it, and insurers and fire inspectors often ask for cleaning records. The standard applies to restaurants, hotels, schools, hospitals and any commercial cooking operation that produces grease-laden vapors.

How often inspection is required

NFPA 96 sets inspection frequency by type and volume of cooking. Systems serving solid fuel cooking, such as wood or charcoal, are inspected monthly. High-volume operations, such as 24-hour cooking, charbroiling or wok cooking, are inspected quarterly. Moderate-volume operations are inspected semiannually, and low-volume operations, such as churches, day camps and seasonal businesses, annually. When an inspection finds the system contaminated with deposits from grease-laden vapors, the entire system must be cleaned by a properly trained, qualified and certified person or company acceptable to the authority having jurisdiction.

What a complete cleaning includes

A proper job cleans the whole system, not just the visible hood. That includes the hood interior, filters, plenum, horizontal and vertical ductwork, access panels and the exhaust fan on the roof. Cleaning is usually done by scraping heavy deposits, then applying degreaser and hot water pressure washing, with plastic sheeting to direct wash water into containers. Ducts without enough access panels cannot be fully cleaned, so a contractor should report areas that could not be reached. Cleaning should bring surfaces to bare metal. Polishing only the hood exterior does not meet the standard.

Certification and documentation

NFPA 96 does not name a single certification, but the authority having jurisdiction decides who is acceptable. Common credentials come from organizations such as the International Kitchen Exhaust Cleaning Association and the Phil Ackland certification program. Some states and cities also license or register exhaust cleaners. After cleaning, the contractor should place a label in a visible location showing the date, the company name and any areas not cleaned. Ask for a written report with before-and-after photos, which helps with fire inspections and insurance reviews.

Pricing and scheduling

Hood cleaning is usually priced per hood system or per visit, with cost driven by the number of hoods, duct length, number of fans, roof access and grease load. Kitchens that have gone a long time between cleanings often require extra labor on the first visit. Work is done after hours when cooking has stopped. Arrange for staff to clear cooking equipment, and confirm the contractor will protect floors and equipment and remove wash water properly. A contract with scheduled visits based on the NFPA 96 frequency keeps the system on track.

Checklist

  • Determine your cooking volume and the matching NFPA 96 inspection frequency
  • Confirm the contractor is acceptable to your local fire authority
  • Require cleaning of hood, filters, plenum, ducts and roof fan
  • Ask how inaccessible duct sections will be reported
  • Require a dated service label and a written report with photos
  • Plan after-hours access and protection of kitchen equipment
  • Confirm wash water collection and disposal
