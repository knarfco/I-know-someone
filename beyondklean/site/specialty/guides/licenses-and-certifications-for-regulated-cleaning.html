Licenses and certifications for regulated cleaning work

An overview of the federal rules and credentials behind asbestos, lead, mold, biohazard, hazmat and radiological cleaning, and how states differ.

Why credentials matter for regulated work

Some cleaning work disturbs materials that federal or state law treats as hazardous. In these cases the contractor's training, licensing and work practices are set by regulation, and a property owner can share liability when unqualified workers are used. The credential tells you a worker or firm has met a minimum training standard. It does not guarantee quality, so it should be paired with experience and references. Federal rules set a baseline, and many states add their own licensing, notification and fee requirements. Requirements vary by state; check your state's licensing board or environmental agency before hiring.

Asbestos and lead

Asbestos work in buildings is governed by the EPA asbestos NESHAP, 40 CFR Part 61 Subpart M, which covers inspection and notification before renovation and demolition, and OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1101 for worker protection in construction. Workers and supervisors are usually trained under the EPA Model Accreditation Plan, and most states license asbestos contractors and individuals. Lead-based paint work falls under EPA rules at 40 CFR Part 745. The Renovation, Repair and Painting rule requires certified firms and renovators for work in pre-1978 housing and child-occupied facilities, while lead abatement requires separate certification. OSHA 29 CFR 1926.62 protects workers on construction jobs involving lead.

Mold and biohazards

There is no general federal license for mold remediation. EPA publishes guidance, and the IICRC S520 standard describes industry practice. Several states, including Texas, New York and Florida, license mold assessors or remediators, and some limit one company from both assessing and remediating the same project. Biohazard and trauma scene cleanup is governed mainly by the OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens standard, 29 CFR 1910.1030, which requires an exposure control plan, training, PPE and hepatitis B vaccination offers. Regulated medical waste is defined and controlled by states, and some states also register or license crime scene cleanup companies.

Hazardous materials and radiological work

Workers at hazardous waste cleanup sites and certain emergency responses need training under OSHA HAZWOPER, 29 CFR 1910.120, typically 40 hours for general site workers plus annual refreshers. Hazardous waste generated during cleaning is regulated under RCRA, and shipping it requires DOT hazmat training and proper manifests. Radiological decontamination is controlled by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission or, in Agreement States, the state radiation control program. Contractors handling licensed radioactive material need the appropriate license or must work under the facility licensee's program, with radiation worker training and dosimetry.

How to check a contractor's credentials

Ask for copies of company licenses and individual certificates for the workers and supervisors who will be on your job, not only for the company owner. Check expiration dates, since many certifications require annual refresher training. Verify licenses on the state agency's public lookup where one exists. Ask how the contractor tracks training and medical clearance, such as respirator fit tests and medical evaluations. For multi-state work, confirm the contractor holds credentials in the state where the job is, because most state licenses do not transfer.

Checklist

  • Identify which regulated materials or hazards the job may involve
  • Look up your state's licensing and notification rules for that work
  • Request company licenses and individual worker and supervisor certificates
  • Check expiration dates and refresher training
  • Verify licenses on the issuing agency's lookup
  • Confirm respirator fit testing and medical clearance where respirators are used
  • Confirm who handles waste characterization, manifests and transport
