High-rise window and facade access safety

How OSHA 1910.27, ANSI/IWCA I-14.1 and rope access practices apply to window and facade cleaning, and what building owners must provide.

The safety rules that apply

Window and facade cleaning on taller buildings is high-risk work. OSHA 29 CFR 1910.27 covers scaffolds and rope descent systems used in general industry, and broader fall protection rules sit in 1910 Subpart D. The ANSI/IWCA I-14.1 Window Cleaning Safety Standard, developed by the International Window Cleaning Association, sets industry practice for equipment, anchorages, rigging, training and work planning. Some states and cities have their own rules for building maintenance equipment and window cleaning. Building owners and contractors share responsibility, so both should understand these requirements before work is scheduled.

Rope descent systems and anchorages

A rope descent system, often called a bosun's chair or controlled descent, lets one worker descend a facade on a rope. OSHA 1910.27 requires that before any such system is used, the building owner inform the employer in writing that anchorages have been identified, tested, certified and maintained. Each anchorage must be capable of supporting at least 5,000 pounds in any direction for each worker attached. Rope descent systems are limited to heights of 300 feet or less above grade unless the employer can show that a safer method is not feasible. Each worker needs an independent fall arrest system on a separate anchorage.

Suspended platforms and building maintenance units

Many tall buildings have permanently installed building maintenance units, davits or powered platforms. These require regular inspection and maintenance under OSHA rules and, often, local codes. Contractors using them should review the building's equipment records, operating procedures and load limits. Workers need training on the specific equipment. Where permanent equipment is not available, contractors may rig temporary suspended scaffolds, which must be designed and erected by competent persons. Wind, weather and building features such as setbacks affect which method is safest.

Rope access

Industrial rope access is a work method in which trained technicians use two independent rope systems, one for working and one for backup. It is used for facade inspection, cleaning and repair where other methods are difficult. Technicians are trained and certified through programs such as SPRAT or IRATA, which define skill levels and require supervision by experienced technicians. Rope access is not the same as a single-rope bosun's chair. Buyers should ask which method a contractor plans to use and what certification the crew holds.

What building owners should prepare

Owners should keep current anchorage certifications, a roof plan showing anchor locations, and records for any permanent access equipment. Plan safe roof access, protection for roof membranes and coordination with tenants and building security. Ask contractors for a written fall protection and rescue plan, equipment inspection records, worker training and certifications, and insurance. Define how sidewalks and entrances below the work will be protected with barriers or closures. Clear communication on the day of work helps prevent conflicts with other building activity.

Checklist

  • Keep written anchorage certification required by OSHA 1910.27
  • Maintain records for davits, BMUs and other access equipment
  • Ask which access method the contractor will use and why
  • Require a written fall protection and rescue plan
  • Confirm IWCA, SPRAT or IRATA training as applicable
  • Plan protection of sidewalks, entrances and roof surfaces
  • Set weather limits and day-of-work communication
