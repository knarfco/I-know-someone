Confined space cleaning basics: OSHA 1910.146

What makes a space permit-required under OSHA 1910.146, how cleaning crews enter safely, and what host employers and contractors each must do.

What counts as a confined space

OSHA defines a confined space as one that is large enough for a worker to enter, has limited means of entry or exit, and is not designed for continuous occupancy. Tanks, silos, vessels, pits, vaults, boilers, sewers and large ducts often qualify. Under 29 CFR 1910.146, a permit-required confined space also has one or more serious hazards, such as a hazardous atmosphere, material that can engulf a worker, a shape that can trap someone, or any other serious safety hazard. Cleaning frequently creates or adds hazards, such as chemical vapors, disturbed residues and low oxygen.

The permit system

Entry into a permit space requires a written program and an entry permit. The permit lists the space, purpose, date and duration, authorized entrants, attendants and entry supervisor, hazards and how they are controlled, isolation measures, test results and rescue arrangements. Before entry, the atmosphere is tested in order for oxygen, then flammable gases and vapors, then toxic contaminants, and testing continues as conditions change. Energy sources and process lines are isolated, often under lockout/tagout. Ventilation is set up as needed. Construction sites follow a similar standard, 29 CFR 1926 Subpart AA.

Roles and rescue

Each permit entry has defined roles. Entrants do the work and know the hazards. The attendant stays outside, keeps count of entrants, monitors conditions and summons rescue but does not enter. The entry supervisor checks that conditions are acceptable and authorizes the permit. Rescue must be planned before entry, either with an on-site team or an outside service that can respond in time and has practiced in similar spaces. Non-entry rescue using retrieval lines and mechanical devices is preferred where practical. Many confined space deaths involve would-be rescuers, so untrained entry must be prevented.

Host employer and contractor duties

When a contractor cleans a permit space, the host employer must inform the contractor of the permit spaces, their hazards and the host's experience with them, and of any precautions the host has used. The host and contractor must coordinate entry when both have workers in or near the space and debrief after the work. The contractor must have its own compliant program, trained workers and equipment, and must tell the host about any hazards it creates or finds. Clear agreement on who issues permits and who provides rescue avoids gaps.

Choosing a contractor for confined space cleaning

Ask to see the contractor's written confined space program, training records for entrants, attendants and supervisors, and calibration records for gas monitors. Ask how it ventilates spaces, what PPE and respiratory protection it uses, and how it handles flammable or toxic residues. Confirm its rescue plan and whether rescue is done in-house or through a service. For petroleum tanks, ask about experience with ANSI/API Standard 2015. Pricing usually reflects crew size including standby attendants, equipment such as vacuum trucks and blowers, waste volume and the time needed for testing and permits.

Checklist

  • Identify and label permit-required confined spaces on site
  • Share known hazards and past experience with the contractor
  • Agree on who issues permits and who provides rescue
  • Review the contractor's written program and training records
  • Confirm gas monitor calibration and testing sequence
  • Plan isolation, lockout and ventilation for each space
  • Hold a debrief after entry and record any new hazards
