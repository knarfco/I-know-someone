Planning industrial shutdown and turnaround cleaning

How plants plan cleaning for shutdowns and turnarounds: early scoping, critical path, permits, staffing, waste and restart readiness.

Start planning early

Cleaning is often on the critical path of a shutdown or turnaround. Vessels must be emptied and cleaned before inspection, repair or catalyst change can start, and the plant cannot restart until cleaning is complete. Large sites begin scoping months ahead. Walk down each piece of equipment with operations and maintenance, record what it contains and how it will be cleaned, and identify what equipment, water, power and waste capacity the work needs. Early contractor involvement lets bidders reserve crews and specialized equipment such as hydroblasting units, vacuum trucks and dry ice systems, which are in high demand during peak turnaround seasons.

Choose methods for each item

Different equipment needs different methods. Heat exchangers are often hydroblasted, tanks may be cleaned with robotic or manual methods, ducts and collectors are vacuumed, and production equipment may be cleaned with dry ice or solvents. The method affects schedule, waste volume and safety. High-pressure water jetting carries serious injury risk and should follow recognized practices such as the WJTA-IMCA recommended practices. Ask contractors to propose methods for each item and to estimate time and waste produced. Where possible, decide in advance what to do if more residue is found than expected.

Permits, isolation and safety

Most turnaround cleaning requires equipment isolation under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147, confined space entry under 1910.146 and often hot work permits. In plants covered by process safety management, 29 CFR 1910.119, contractors must be informed of process hazards and follow the plant's safe work practices, and the plant must evaluate contractor safety performance. Plan who issues permits, how gas testing is done, and how rescue is provided. Schedule site orientation and badging before the shutdown starts. Turnarounds bring many crews into one area, so coordinate work to avoid conflicts between trades.

Staffing and logistics

Turnarounds often run around the clock. Contractors need enough qualified workers for day and night shifts and a plan for fatigue. Confirm staging areas, laydown space, water supply and discharge points, power and lighting. Arrange waste containers in advance and know where each waste stream will go. Daily planning meetings help track progress against the schedule and spot delays early. Ask contractors to report progress by equipment item so the turnaround team can adjust the sequence of other work. Agree in advance on how added scope will be approved during the event.

Closeout and restart

Before restart, each cleaned item should be inspected and accepted. Record the condition found, the work done and any damage or problems noted for maintenance. Make sure all tools, hoses, temporary equipment and debris are removed from vessels and lines. In PSM-covered processes, the pre-startup safety review confirms equipment is ready to return to service. Collect waste manifests and disposal records. After the event, review what worked and what did not, and use that to plan the next shutdown. Record actual durations and waste volumes for each item so future estimates are better.

Checklist

  • Walk down every item with operations and record contents and access
  • Assign a cleaning method and estimate time and waste for each item
  • Book contractors and specialized equipment early
  • Plan isolation, confined space, hot work permits and rescue
  • Complete orientation and badging before the shutdown starts
  • Arrange water, power, staging, waste containers and disposal routes
  • Track progress by item in daily planning meetings
  • Inspect, accept and document each item before restart
