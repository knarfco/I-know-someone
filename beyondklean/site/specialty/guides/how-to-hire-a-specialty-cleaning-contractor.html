Specialty · Buyer's guide

How to hire a specialty cleaning contractor

A step-by-step approach to defining the job, screening contractors, comparing bids and setting up a specialty cleaning project.

Define the problem before calling anyone

Start by writing down what needs to be cleaned, why, and what result you expect. A contamination event, a fire code finding, a shutdown and a pre-occupancy clean are different jobs with different contractors. List the areas, equipment and materials involved, the known hazards and the time you have. If you do not know whether a regulated material such as asbestos, lead or mold is present, plan for testing first. Testing by an independent party gives every bidder the same facts and keeps the cleaning contractor from deciding the size of its own job. A clear problem statement is the single most useful thing a buyer can give a contractor.

Find contractors with matching experience

Specialty cleaning covers very different trades. A company that cleans data halls may not clean storage tanks, and a duct cleaner may not handle kitchen exhaust. Look for contractors who do your specific type of work regularly, in your industry and at your scale. Ask for two or three similar projects completed in the last few years, with the type of facility, size and outcome. Ask for references you can actually call. Contractors who list every possible service without examples of any may subcontract the work you need, which is not always a problem but should be disclosed.

Check credentials, insurance and safety records

For regulated work, the license or certification is a legal requirement, not a preference. Ask for copies and confirm them with the issuing agency where possible. Request a certificate of insurance showing general liability, workers' compensation and, where relevant, pollution liability and auto coverage. Ask for the company's written safety program and its OSHA recordable and lost-time rates for recent years. Many industrial owners also ask for an experience modification rate. If the contractor will use subcontractors, require the same documents from them.

Compare bids on the same scope

Bids are only comparable when they price the same work. Send every contractor the same written scope, drawings, photos and site rules, and hold one walkthrough for all bidders. Ask each bid to list what is included and excluded, the crew size and schedule, equipment, waste handling and the method of verifying results. Specialty work is commonly priced per unit, per day or as a lump sum, so ask contractors to show how they reached their number. A low bid that excludes waste disposal, access equipment or overtime is often the most expensive once the work starts.

Set up the contract and the job

Put the scope, schedule, price basis, change order process and acceptance criteria in writing. State who obtains permits, who handles waste and who pays for any third-party testing. Agree on daily reporting, photo documentation and a single point of contact on each side. Before the start date, hold a pre-job meeting to review hazards, access, lockout procedures, emergency plans and site rules. At closeout, require the documents you will need later, such as waste manifests, clearance results or cleaning reports.

Checklist

  • Write a problem statement with areas, hazards, deadlines and expected result
  • Arrange independent testing when regulated materials may be present
  • Shortlist contractors with recent, similar projects and callable references
  • Collect licenses, certifications, insurance certificates and safety records
  • Send one written scope and hold one walkthrough for all bidders
  • Compare inclusions, exclusions, crew, schedule and waste handling, not just totals
  • Put acceptance criteria and closeout documents in the contract
