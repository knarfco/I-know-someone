Commercial duct cleaning and the NADCA ACR standard

How commercial HVAC cleaning works under ACR, the NADCA Standard: assessment, source removal, containment, verification and buyer questions.

What the ACR standard covers

ACR, the NADCA Standard for Assessment, Cleaning and Restoration of HVAC Systems, is published by the National Air Duct Cleaners Association. The current edition is 2025. It sets procedures for inspecting HVAC systems, deciding when cleaning is needed, performing the cleaning, controlling contamination and verifying that the system is clean. It covers air handlers, coils, fans, plenums and supply and return ducts. Building owners and engineers often cite ACR in specifications so that bidders work to the same method and acceptance tests.

Assessment first

A sound project starts with an inspection of the system. Inspectors look at representative sections of ductwork and components to see what is present, such as dust, debris, microbial growth or fire residue. The assessment helps decide whether cleaning is needed and where, and it notes hazards such as asbestos-containing insulation or damaged components. In some buildings, cleaning only the air handler and nearby ducts solves the problem. In others, the full system needs work. The assessment results should drive the scope rather than a contractor's standard package.

Source removal and containment

ACR relies on source removal: mechanically dislodging debris and capturing it under negative pressure so it does not spread into occupied space. Contractors use HEPA-filtered vacuum collection, rotary brushes, air whips and hand cleaning, and cut access openings where needed and then seal them. Coils are cleaned with appropriate coil cleaners and rinsed. Containment and work area protection are especially important in hospitals, labs and occupied offices. Antimicrobial products should be used only when needed and as labeled, after cleaning, not instead of it.

Verification

After cleaning, the system should be checked. ACR describes visual inspection and, when there is a dispute or the specification requires it, the NADCA Vacuum Test, a gravimetric method that measures debris remaining on a duct surface. The Surface Comparison Test is another option. Photos and video of before and after conditions at the same locations help document the result. Buyers can also hire an independent party to verify cleanliness on larger projects, which removes the contractor from judging its own work.

Credentials and buyer questions

NADCA certifies individuals as Air Systems Cleaning Specialists (ASCS), and NADCA member companies must employ at least one ASCS. Ask who will supervise your job and whether they hold the ASCS credential. Ask how the contractor will access each part of the system, how it will protect occupants and finishes, and how it will handle insulation or lined ductwork. Pricing is commonly based on system size, number of air handlers and registers, duct length, access difficulty and work hours. Night and weekend work in occupied buildings adds cost.

Checklist

  • Reference ACR, the NADCA Standard, in your specification
  • Start with an assessment of the system's condition
  • Check for asbestos-containing insulation before work begins
  • Require HEPA-filtered negative pressure source removal
  • Define containment and occupant protection
  • Agree on verification methods and photo documentation
  • Confirm an ASCS-certified supervisor will be on the job
