# Fair Rates, Risky Structure: Rebuild Before Signing

The economics of the proposed Beyond Klean license are broadly within market norms. The legal structure and the exclusivity terms are not, and both need rebuilding before any operator signs. The **$368.71 weekly fee (about $1,598 a month, $19,173 a year)** is cheap for a national exclusive brand license. It is expensive if all it buys is a lead website, and it is only fair if the brand delivers a steady flow of qualified jobs. The **10%/7%/4% commission on brand-generated jobs only** is lighter than commercial cleaning franchises, which take 10–15%+ of every billed dollar including the operator's own accounts. It is heavier than construction referral fees, which run about 0.75–5%. Charging the fixed fee and the commission together pushes the operator's effective cost on brand jobs past typical cleaning net margins at low volume. The legal problem is bigger than the pricing. A trademark license, plus a required payment that passes the FTC's **$735** six-month exemption threshold in week two, plus website, marketing and lead support, makes this very likely a "franchise" under the FTC Franchise Rule. Trimming the payments to dodge that rule pushes the deal toward the Business Opportunity Rule, because providing customers is itself a trigger. The recommended fixes are to credit the weekly fee against commissions, apply the tiers marginally rather than to the whole job, define lead attribution and recurring-contract treatment, replace "no fixed term" with a short, earned exclusivity period backed by performance standards, and either comply with the FTC Rule or restructure with franchise counsel. *This report is legal information, not legal advice. Every figure comes from search-engine summaries of the cited pages, because full pages could not be opened during research. Several sources are aggregators or promoters that disagree with each other, so verify every number against primary documents before relying on it.*

## The weekly fee is cheap exclusivity but pricey lead rental

How fair the weekly fee is depends on what it is meant to buy. The deal mixes three products: a national brand and territory right, a lead-generation website, and an estimating tool and back office. Each has a different market price.

**As the price of territorial exclusivity, $19,173 a year for all 50 states, D.C. and the territories is very low.** In janitorial franchising, regional master rights are priced by population. Jani-King's regional master initial fee has been reported at about **$50,000 per 100,000 population, with a $100,000 minimum** ([FDD Exchange](https://fddexchange.com/view-fdd-docs/jani-king-regional-master-franchise-2018-fdd-summary/)). Jan-Pro's regional developer tier requires a total investment of **$130,000–$421,500** and pays the franchisor a 4% royalty on revenue ([Franchise Chatter](https://www.franchisechatter.com/2024/11/05/fdd-talk-jan-pro-regional-developer-franchise-franchise-costs-fees-average-revenues-and-or-profits-2024-review/)). Even the smallest regional master minimum is more than five years of this weekly fee. The comparison is imperfect, since those masters get a full operating system and sub-franchising rights. Still, it shows the brand is granting exclusivity cheaply. That makes a strong case for securing exclusivity through performance obligations rather than a higher fee.

**As rent for a lead website, $1,598 a month sits at the upper-middle of the market.** Rank-and-rent practitioners report flat rents of roughly $500–$1,500 a month for mid-tier trades and $1,000–$3,000 for premium trades such as water damage and roofing ([Masum AK](https://masumak.com/how-much-to-charge-for-rank-and-rent/)). In one practitioner's tracked launches, sites producing **31–45 qualified leads a month rented for $1,100–$1,900**, while sites producing 10–14 leads rented for $300–$550 ([LocusPilot](https://locuspilot.com/blog/rank-and-rent-website-examples)). If the fee is justified as website rent, the brand should expect to deliver on the order of 30–45 qualified leads a month. All of these figures are self-reported by people selling rank-and-rent courses, and none are specific to construction cleaning, so treat them as directional.

The fee's structure has its own issues. A day-one weekly charge is the feature that creates the federal franchise problem discussed below. The "price-protected for six months, then quarterly revenue-based reviews" mechanism is also unusual. Contract escalators are normally annual and tied to CPI. A "lesser of CPI or X%" clause acts as a true cap that protects the payer, while "greater of CPI or X%" turns X% into a guaranteed minimum raise ([Law Insider](https://www.lawinsider.com/clause/fee-escalation); [Zuva](https://zuva.ai/contract-central/inflation-adjustment/)). A quarterly review based on undefined "revenue" gives the operator no way to forecast costs, and it invites disputes over whose revenue counts.

## The 10/7/4 commission sits between referral fees and franchise royalties

The commission applies only to jobs Beyond Klean generates, and the operator keeps 100% of its own customers. That makes the structure lighter than every unit-franchise janitorial model in the research. Those models charge their percentage on all billings, for the life of each account, and add a separate one-time finder's fee for accounts the franchisor supplies.

The aggregator figures conflict in places. For example, Jan-Pro unit royalties are reported at both 10% and 13%, and Coverall's split is reported both as 5% + 10% and as a flat 15%. Check them against each brand's FDD Item 6.

Against this spread, **the 10% tier on jobs under $25,000 is market-rate for a cleaning brand that does more than pass along a phone number.** It equals what cleaning owners pay each other for referrals, sits inside rank-and-rent revenue-share ranges, is half of Coverall's 20% special-services finder's fee, and is at or below a single janitorial royalty line before admin fees. **Measured purely as a construction referral fee, 10% is two to four times market, and 7% is above market.** The **4% tier on jobs over $100,000** is within the upper construction referral range of 2–5% ([NSPE](https://www.nspe.org/node/9569); [BuildZoom](https://www.buildzoom.com/answers/t/who-pays-the-referral-fee-to-buildzoom/2480)). The declining shape matches common practice: rank-and-rent operators quote lower percentages for higher-ticket trades, and sales plans pay lower rates on large deals. No published standard exists for the specific breakpoints, so $25,000 and $100,000 are negotiable rather than benchmarked.

Two drafting gaps matter more than the rates themselves. The first is the **tier cliff**. If each tier applies to the whole job, a $24,999 job pays $2,500 in commission, while a $25,000 job pays $1,750. That rewards the operator for pricing just over each breakpoint and makes the brand worse off on bigger jobs. Marginal brackets solve this: 10% on the first $25,000, 7% on the next $75,000 and 4% above $100,000. Under that approach, a $100,000 job pays $7,750 (7.75%), and a $200,000 job pays $11,750 (5.9%). The second is **recurring janitorial work**, which the proposal does not address. Janitorial franchisors keep 10–15% for the life of an account, while sales reps typically earn the equivalent of about 4–15% of first-year contract value. Lifetime residuals are generally considered expensive and are commonly capped at 12–24 months ([Commissionly](https://www.commissionly.io/residual-commission/)). A defensible middle ground is the tier rate on the first 12 months of a recurring contract's billing, a reduced residual (for example 5%) for months 13–24, and nothing after that. This mirrors the posted plan that pays 50% of month one, then 10% for 12 months, then 5% for 12 more ([Gusto posting](https://jobs.gusto.com/postings/choice-facility-services-llc-sales-representative-60430cf4-5ac5-4384-9ca1-b7a6ad36aa93)).

## Stacking the fee on the commission can consume the operator's margin

None of the lead-generation benchmarks charge a fixed fee and a revenue share together. Rank-and-rent sources present rent and revenue share as **alternatives** ([LocusPilot](https://locuspilot.com/blog/what-is-rank-and-rent)), and construction finder's fees are one-time and paid only on success, with no recurring charge. Combining the two looks like a franchise package. That is acceptable only if the brand delivers franchise-like value.

Margins make the stacking concrete. IBISWorld puts U.S. janitorial services profit at about **11.9%**, with wages at 43.2% of revenue ([CMM Online](https://cmmonline.com/news/u-s-janitorial-services-market-projected-to-grow-nearly-2-in-2026)). Blog and calculator sources put commercial cleaning net margins at about 10–20%, and post-construction gross margins at 30–50% before overhead ([ZenMaid](https://get.zenmaid.com/tools/profit-margin-calculator); [Convertlabs](https://blog.convertlabs.io/how-profitable-is-a-cleaning-business-in-2026)). The table assumes every brand-generated job falls in the 10% tier.

| Annual brand-generated job volume | 10% commission | Weekly fees (52 weeks) | Total paid | Effective take on brand jobs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $50,000 | $5,000 | $19,173 | $24,173 | 48.3% |
| $100,000 | $10,000 | $19,173 | $29,173 | 29.2% |
| $150,000 | $15,000 | $19,173 | $34,173 | 22.8% |
| $250,000 | $25,000 | $19,173 | $44,173 | 17.7% |
| $500,000 | $50,000 | $19,173 | $69,173 | 13.8% |
| $1,000,000 | $100,000 | $19,173 | $119,173 | 11.9% |

Below roughly $250,000 a year of brand-generated work, the operator's combined cost on those jobs likely exceeds its entire net margin on them. Only above about $500,000 does the effective rate settle into the 10–15% band that janitorial franchisors charge. A fair counterpoint is that the weekly fee also buys rights the operator uses on its own commission-free customers, so the brand-job percentage overstates the burden for an operator with a large existing book. With $1 million of total revenue, the fee is under 2% of sales. The fee breaks even with a 10% commission at **about $191,700 of brand jobs a year**. That is the natural point to **credit the weekly fee against commissions**, so the operator pays the greater of the two rather than the sum. The brand keeps a guaranteed floor, and the operator stops paying twice for the same leads. If the fee stays separate, the commission should come down, or the fee should be explicitly tied to a minimum number of qualified leads.

Lead attribution is the other economic hinge. Because commissions apply only to brand-generated jobs, the operator has a direct incentive to reclassify brand leads as its own. Commission reports, read access to the CRM or invoicing system, and audit rights are standard protection. Franchise agreements commonly shift audit costs to the payer if revenue is understated by more than **2%** ([SEC EDGAR, franchise agreement](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001081899/000095012310031818/h69192exv10w4.htm)), and IP licenses more often use **5%** ([SEC EDGAR, license](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000320017/000032001716000119/exhibit103a.htm)). Interest on underpayments typically runs 1–1.5% a month, capped at the legal maximum ([SEC EDGAR](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000785815/000095013407020570/d50173exv10w1.htm)). The contract should define a brand lead by source, such as tracked phone numbers, website forms, estimator submissions and brand vendor registrations. It should also set an attribution window, such as 12 months from first contact, so repeat and follow-on work from a brand customer stays commissionable.

## A day-one weekly fee very likely makes this a federal franchise

The FTC Franchise Rule (16 CFR Part 436) applies when three elements are present: a trademark license, significant control or assistance, and a required payment ([Fox Rothschild](https://www.foxrothschild.com/publications/whats-an-accidental-franchise-and-could-i-be-one)). Labels in the contract do not matter, because regulators look at economic substance ([LexBlog](https://www.lexblog.com/?p=1730125)). The trademark element is met here. The payment element is met almost immediately. Since July 12, 2024, the minimum-payment exemption covers only deals with required payments **under $735** from before opening through the first six months of operation ([Federal Register, 89 FR 57078](https://govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-07-12/pdf/2024-14933.pdf); [FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/85862)). Two weekly payments total **$737.42**, and the first six months total about **$9,586**. Commissions count as well, since FTC staff presume that expected payments, such as revenue shares in an industry where typical revenue makes them likely, meet the minimum ([FTC Advisory Opinion 99-1](https://ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/advisory-opinions/informal-staff-advisory-opinion-99-1)). The only open element is whether the website, estimating tool, marketing and lead support amount to "significant" assistance. A court found an accidental franchise in a "license" built on similar marketing support and requirements ([Lathrop GPM on *Safe Step Walk In Tub v. CKH Industries*](https://www.lathropgpm.com/insights/bathtub-manufacturers-licensing-arrangement-created-an-accidental-franchise-but-court-finds-limited-remedies)).

The other exemptions do not fit a typical operator. The large-investment exemption requires **$1,469,600**, and the large-franchisee exemption requires **$7,348,000** in net worth plus five years in business ([McDonald Hopkins](https://mcdonaldhopkins.com/insights/news/ftc-publishes-new-monetary-thresholds-for-three-exemptions-to-the-franchise-rule)). The **fractional franchise** exemption is the only realistic one. It requires an operator with more than two years in the same business, for whom the licensed line is expected to be no more than 20% of first-year sales ([Aaron Hall](https://aaronhall.com/fractional-franchise/)). An established construction-cleaning company adding the brand as a sideline might qualify. An operator running its whole business under the brand would not. The FTC Rule gives no private right of action ([Lathrop GPM](https://www.lathropgpm.com/insights/bathtub-manufacturers-licensing-arrangement-created-an-accidental-franchise-but-court-finds-limited-remedies)), but FTC civil penalties reach **$53,088 per violation** under the January 2025 adjustment ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/87476)).

The obvious workaround is to waive all fees and commissions for six months. FTC staff have said fees payable more than six months after operations begin appeared to fall within the exemption, while warning against devices meant to circumvent the Rule ([FTC Advisory Opinion 98-3](https://search.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/advisory-opinions/informal-staff-advisory-opinion-98-3)). A 1995 staff opinion approved a six-month no-payment period for a sub-agent ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/33858)). That route has a trap. The **Business Opportunity Rule (16 CFR Part 437) has no dollar threshold**, and it applies when a seller represents that it will provide "outlets, accounts or customers" ([Offit Kurman](https://www.offitkurman.com/offit-kurman-blogs/whats-a-biz-op)). It expressly reaches franchises that escape Part 436 through the minimum-payment exemption ([GPO, 16 CFR 437.8](https://www.gpo.gov/fdsys/pkg/CFR-2014-title16-vol1/pdf/CFR-2014-title16-vol1-sec437-8.pdf)). "Brand-generated jobs" is close to a promise of customers. Deferring payments therefore likely trades an FDD for the Business Opportunity Rule's one-page disclosure. Part 437.8 still cites a $500 threshold rather than $735, which leaves a gray zone for counsel to resolve. The Franchise Rule's 14-day FDD delivery period and the Business Opportunity Rule's 7-day waiting period come from background knowledge, not a source retrieved for this report.

The **$45-an-hour optional marketing and vendor-registration help** should stay genuinely optional and be documented that way. The "required payment" element is read broadly enough to capture advertising and assistance fees ([Ontario Bar Association](https://oba.org/Sections/Franchise-Law/Articles/Articles-2018/January-2018/The-Danger-of-the-Accidental-Franchise)), and the service itself adds to the "assistance" side of the test.

Litigation history adds a further point. The janitorial misclassification cases against Coverall in Massachusetts and Jan-Pro in California focused on franchisors that billed customers and **deducted royalties, management fees and additional-business fees from the receipts** ([Lathrop GPM](https://www.lathropgpm.com/insights/massachusetts-supreme-court-issues-damages-ruling-in-awuah/); [Buchalter](https://www.buchalter.com/insights/roman-v-jan-pro-franchising-international-inc-court-clarifies-employee-misclassification-and-abc-test-application)). In those cases the control-plus-deduction structure drew the challenge, not the fee level. The current proposal, where the operator bills its own customers and pays the brand separately, avoids that pattern and should keep doing so. Separately, some states restrict contractors from paying referral fees: California makes contractor-to-contractor kickbacks a misdemeanor, but permits referral services that do not hold themselves out as performing the work ([CSLB](https://cslb.ca.gov/Newsletter/2015-16%20winter/referral_entities.asp)). A 50-state grant needs a state-by-state check of contractor licensing rules.

### Florida, Georgia and Alabama layer on filings and deceptive-practice exposure

**Florida** does not register franchises. A franchisor that meets the FTC definition must file an annual exemption notice with the Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services, using **Form 10500 with a $100 fee**, before selling in the state ([flrules.org, Fla. Stat. 559.802](https://flrules.org/gateway/statute.asp?id=559.802); [Fla. Admin. Code 5J-10.002](https://regulations.justia.com/states/florida/5/5j/chapter-5j-10/section-5j-10-002)). Without that filing, the deal risks falling under the Sale of Business Opportunities Act. That Act covers an initial fee **over $500** paired with listed seller representations, though the sales-or-marketing-program representation is carved out when it accompanies the license of a **registered** trademark ([FindLaw, Fla. Stat. 559.801](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xxxiii-regulation-of-trade-commerce-investments-and-solicitations/fl-st-sect-559-801/)). The Florida Franchise Act (Fla. Stat. 817.416) separately makes intentional misrepresentation of success prospects or total required investment actionable, with return of all money invested plus attorney's fees ([FindLaw](https://corporate.findlaw.com/business-operations/the-florida-franchise-act.html)). Its definition turns on reliance for the basic supply of goods, which a service-only license may not meet.

**Georgia** also has no franchise registration. Its Sale of Business Opportunities Act (O.C.G.A. 10-1-410) uses a **$500** initial-payment test, and its marketing-program prong likewise excludes programs offered with a registered mark ([Onecle](https://law.onecle.com/georgia/title-10/10-1-410.html)). Practitioners read that carve-out as favoring federally registered marks ([Internicola Law](https://www.franchiselawsolutions.com/franchising/georgia-franchise-registration)). Sellers promising refunds or buy-backs need a **$75,000** bond or trust account ([Onecle, 10-1-412](https://law.onecle.com/georgia/title-10/10-1-412.html)). **Alabama** has no franchise registration or disclosure law ([Internicola Law](https://franchiselawsolutions.com/franchising/alabama-franchise-registration)), and research found no Alabama business-opportunity statute, though no source confirmed that absence outright. Alabama exposure runs through the federal Rule and the Deceptive Trade Practices Act, which allows enforcement by the Attorney General and private damages ([USLegal](https://businesslaw.uslegal.com/deceptive-trade-practices-laws/alabama-deceptive-trade-practices-laws/)). Two points cut across all three states: **federal registration of the brand mark materially reduces business-opportunity risk** in Florida and Georgia, and the Georgia and Alabama code texts found were older compilations. A national grant also reaches states with their own registration and relationship laws, and states may not honor federal exemptions ([LexBlog](https://www.lexblog.com/?p=1730125)). This research did not cover those states.

## National exclusivity must be earned, staged and reversible

No source described an indefinite nationwide exclusive granted to one small service operator as common practice. Market practice makes exclusivity **conditional**, tied to minimum sales, development schedules or purchase minimums, and reversible when those conditions are missed ([Heritage Law](https://www.heritagelawwi.com/franchise-disclosure-document-item-12); [LegalClarity](https://legalclarity.org/what-is-an-area-development-franchise-agreement/); [Law Insider](https://lawinsider.com/clause/minimum-purchase-requirements)). The central risk is "shelving": a licensee with crews in a few metros holds 50 states, the licensor earns nothing elsewhere, and slow service damages the brand ([Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/fr/insights-and-events/insights/2020/07/licensing-out-try-not-to-get-benched)). Janitorial regulators warn the other side as well: when an operator cannot serve a location, the franchisor treats its obligation as met and routes the account elsewhere, so promised revenue never arrives ([Maryland AG](https://www.marylandattorneygeneral.gov/Securities%20Documents/janitorial.pdf)).

The closest models are the **area-developer schedule**, where territories are earned through coverage milestones by date, and the **distributor minimum**, an annual revenue or commission floor. Calibration matters. UK practice reportedly caps targets at about **70% of average franchisee performance**, and systems without performance data often set no targets for the first years ([Franchise World UK](https://www.franchiseworld.co.uk/setting-targets-for-franchisees/)). For a brand without history, that argues for ramped minimums in years one through three. Missed standards should trigger a remedy ladder of notice and cure, then conversion of the affected state or region to non-exclusive, then termination for repeated or monetary defaults. Practitioners treat conversion to non-exclusive as the workhorse remedy, and it must be written down: without an express clause, exclusivity can survive a missed target ([Business-in-a-Box](https://www.business-in-a-box.com/template/exclusive-distribution-agreement-D1240); [Contracko](https://contracko.com/clause-library/exclusivity/index.html.md)). A **shortfall top-up**, where the operator pays the difference to keep exclusivity, is an established alternative ([Perez-Llorca/Lexology](https://perezllorca.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Sub-franchising-Master-Franchising-and-Development-Agents-Lexology.pdf)).

"No fixed term" also departs from practice. Franchise agreements run fixed terms of about 5–10 years with conditional renewal ([CFA](https://cfa.ca/franchisecanada/q-why-is-the-start-of-my-franchise-relationship-the-best-time-to-think-about-the-end-of-it)), and master agreements run 10–20 years with renewal tied to targets ([Franchising.com](https://franchisors.franchising.com/articles/franchising_abroad_the_two_most_common_international_franchise_deals.html)). An open-ended grant leaves neither side with a clean exit or renewal checkpoint. Reversion of rights on termination is normal. Fairness also requires settling who owns the customers the operator sourced, the website, the tracked phone numbers and the brand leads in progress. Franchise disclosure rules also require stating which channels the franchisor reserves and whether the franchisee is paid for sales made through them ([Franchise.law, Item 12](https://franchise.law/franchise-disclosure-document/item-12/)). National multi-site accounts, which janitorial franchisors commonly keep at the franchisor level ([Coverall National Accounts](https://www.coverall.com/national-accounts/)), should be addressed explicitly.

Response-time standards have no contract benchmark. The only data is vendor research on franchise-sales leads, where **only 26% of 500+ brands responded within five minutes** ([FranFunnel](https://franfunnel.com/speed-to-lead)). The windows in the table below are therefore proposals, not norms.

| Term | Current proposal | Recommended adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Legal structure | Weekly fee from day one | Have franchise counsel choose one of three paths: full FTC compliance with an FDD; fractional-franchise eligibility for established operators only; or deferral of all payments past month six, plus a Business Opportunity Rule analysis. ## Conclusion

On price alone, the proposal is close to fair: a cleaning brand charging 10% on small jobs and tapering to 4% is in line with what the industry pays for sourced work, and it is gentler than any unit-franchise janitorial model because the operator's own customers stay commission-free. The real imbalance runs in both directions. The operator pays twice for brand leads, through the fee and the commission, at volumes where that can wipe out its margin. The brand, meanwhile, gives away a national monopoly for less than a single regional master fee and gets no performance guarantee in return. Crediting the fee against commissions and making exclusivity earned rather than granted fixes both sides at once, without changing the headline numbers.

The legal finding should drive the order of work. The weekly-fee design that makes the economics simple is also what makes the deal a likely unregistered franchise, and the obvious workaround of deferring fees runs into the Business Opportunity Rule because the brand promises customers. The decision about which legal structure to use, made with franchise counsel and after verifying every figure here against primary sources, has to come before the fee schedule is finalized. That decision may change the schedule itself, for example if a six-month deferral or an FDD with its own fee disclosures becomes the chosen path.
